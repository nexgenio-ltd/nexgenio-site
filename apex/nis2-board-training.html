In brief

Does NIS2 require board members to be trained?

Yes. Article 20(2) of NIS2 requires Member States to ensure that the members of the management body of essential and important entities follow training, and encourages entities to offer equivalent training to employees regularly. It is written as an obligation on the individuals, which is what separates it from general staff awareness training. The directive names no interval; supervisory practice is converging on an annual cycle, plus a session when a new member joins or the risk profile changes materially.

What a supervisor asks for is the evidence: the date, the agenda, the materials used, who attended and who delivered it. The attendance record is usually requested first, because it shows the duty reached the named individuals rather than the organisation in general.

Source: Article 20 of Directive (EU) 2022/2555 on EUR-Lex. Your operative obligation sits in your own country's transposing law — in the Netherlands, the Cyberbeveiligingswet.

Article 20

Three duties, one of them personal

Article 20 does not ask the management body to receive a briefing. It names approval, competence and accountability as duties of the individuals who sit on it.

Approve the measures

The management body approves the cybersecurity risk management measures and oversees their implementation. Delegating the work is expected. Delegating the accountability is not available.

Be trained for it

Article 20(2) requires members to follow training, so that they can identify risks and assess cybersecurity practices themselves rather than accept assurances from the people they supervise.

Answer for it

Supervisory powers reach the management body directly. Penalties reach €10 million or 2% of worldwide turnover for essential entities, and €7 million or 1.4% for important entities.

The directive also encourages entities to offer similar training to employees on a regular basis. The board duty is the one written as a requirement, which is why it is worth handling as a distinct engagement. See the full NIS2 picture.

The session

What a board actually needs to hear

Half a day, built around your organisation rather than around the directive. The agenda below is the standard shape and it moves to fit the room.

  • Where your organisation sits in scope, and as which tier
  • What the management body is signing when it approves the measures
  • The ten Article 21 measures in governance language, not controls language
  • The 24-hour, 72-hour and one-month reporting clocks, and who starts them
  • How supply chain duties arrive as contract terms rather than as regulation
  • What a supervisor will ask for, and what good evidence looks like
  • The questions a director should be asking the CISO each quarter
  • Where personal exposure begins, and what discharges it
  • A short scenario walked through as a board, not as a technical team
  • An attendance record and agenda you can place in the governance file

Delivery

A governance conversation, not a security briefing

Board training works when the room is engaged rather than briefed. Sessions are facilitated in governance language, built around decisions the management body actually takes, and structured so directors can ask basic questions freely. The measure of a good session is the quality of the questions in the second hour.

The content comes from practitioners who build NIS2 frameworks for a living, so what the board hears is grounded in how the obligations are actually implemented rather than in how the directive reads. Translated into the terms a board decides in.

The facilitator is matched to the board

Who delivers the session is settled on the scoping call. Depending on your sector, your language and the composition of your board, that is either a NexGenio facilitator or a qualified specialist from our accredited partner network. The requirement we hold constant is the qualification and the independence, not the name on the agenda.

Independent of the reporting line

Delivery sits outside the management chain being discussed. That is what lets the evidence stand on its own, and it is also what makes the session useful, because directors can put a question to an outside facilitator that is harder to put to the person who reports to them. Sessions are available in English and in German.

Questions

Frequently asked

Does NIS2 really require board members personally to be trained?
Yes. Article 20(2) states that Member States shall ensure that members of the management body are required to follow training, and encourages entities to offer equivalent training to employees regularly. It is written as an obligation on the individuals, which is what separates it from awareness training delivered to staff.
How often does the training need to happen?
The directive does not name an interval. Supervisory practice across Member States is converging on an annual cycle, with an additional session when a new member joins the board or when the organisation's risk profile changes materially. An annual rhythm also keeps the evidence current, which is the practical test a supervisor applies.
What evidence does a supervisor expect?
The date, the agenda, the materials used, who attended, and who delivered it. In practice the attendance record is the document that gets requested first, because it is what demonstrates that the duty reached the named individuals rather than the organisation in general. Every session produces that pack.
Can our CISO deliver this instead?
A CISO can and should brief the board regularly, and that reporting line is valuable. Article 20 asks something different: it asks the management body to be able to assess the cybersecurity practices it oversees, including the work of the people reporting to it. Independent delivery is what makes the evidence stand on its own, and it also gives directors a setting where they can ask basic questions freely.
Is this available in German?
Yes. Sessions are available in English and in German. Which language works best for your board, and whether the session should be framed against the directive or against a specific national transposition, is one of the things the scoping call establishes.
We have not started our NIS2 programme yet. Is board training premature?
It is usually the opposite. The board approves the measures and sets the budget, so a board that understands the duty early tends to scope the programme correctly the first time. If you want the ground established first, the NIS2 Baseline Check gives you a written determination of scope and exposure that the board session can then build on.
